\begin{abstract}
  Threshold logic --- implementing neural-network-style inner
  products as gates that fire when weighted inputs exceed a
  threshold --- has a long architectural lineage, but its silicon
  implementations have historically required exotic devices: flash
  transistors, memristors, or current-mode analog cells. We show
  that the threshold function decomposes cleanly onto ordinary
  digital standard cells via a chunked-LUT scheme, eliminating the
  dependency on fabrication-specific primitives that has constrained
  threshold-logic accelerators. We demonstrate this at SoC scale
  with TINKER, a sixteen-neuron threshold-logic-mapped tile
  integrated with a PicoRV32 RV32I core, a Wishbone B4 fabric, and
  OpenRAM IMEM/DMEM macros, hosting an entire grouped binary MLP
  through five software-scheduled sequential evaluations. On the
  full 10\,000-image MNIST test set, TINKER reaches 85.60\,\%
  accuracy with a 95\,\% Wilson confidence interval of
  [84.90\,\%, 86.27\,\%], at 8.06\,\textmu J per inference and
  47.21\,mW activity-aware power. Hierarchical activity-aware
  decomposition shows that the threshold-logic compute occupies
  24.3\,\% of total SoC power --- the largest active datapath
  bucket in the system --- validating fully-digital threshold logic
  as a viable primary inference compute element at SoC scale, not
  merely a curiosity confined to specialized fabrication flows.
  TINKER is implemented on the open sky130 flow and is
  sign-off-clean on KLayout DRC, Netgen LVS, KLayout XOR, and
  antenna.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Binary and spiking neural network accelerators have driven dramatic
energy-efficiency gains for inference at the edge, with reported
figures on the order of hundreds of TOp/s/W in advanced commercial
process nodes~\cite{xne2018,yodann2018,chewbaccann2021}. Those
results, however, are concentrated in proprietary PDKs paired with
proprietary memory compilers, which limits independent reproduction
and constrains the methodological rigor that academic groups can
apply to system-level claims. OpenSpike has previously demonstrated SoC-scale neural inference on
sky130 for a spiking payload~\cite{openspike}, but the system-level
question of where SoC power is spent --- and what becomes the
first-order optimisation target once memory is properly architected
--- has not been answered for fully-digital threshold-logic BNN
accelerators. This paper presents TINKER, the system that answers it.

Our approach decomposes the binary inner product
$y_i = \mathbb{1}[\,\mathrm{popcount}(x \xnor w_i) \ge \theta_i]$
into a chunked threshold-logic mapping that Yosys lowers into a
fan-in-bounded combinational tree of \texttt{sky130\_fd\_sc\_hd}
multiplexers and AOI cells. Sixteen such neurons, each with its own
64-bit weight and 7-bit threshold, share a registered 64-bit input
and produce a 16-bit output vector per evaluation. The tile is
embedded in a single-clock RV32I SoC: a PicoRV32 core~\cite{picorv32}
acts as Wishbone master to four slaves --- an OpenRAM IMEM, an
OpenRAM DMEM, the tile peripheral, and a sim-observability GPIO ---
through a flat one-master / four-slave decode. The deployed network
is a grouped binary MLP over a 14\,\(\times\)\,14 input: four
parallel 49\,\(\to\,16\) branches concatenate into a 64-bit hidden
vector that the same tile maps to ten output logits in a fifth
evaluation. Off-chip training uses a hard-tanh straight-through
estimator~\cite{hubara_bnn} and a OneCycleLR
schedule~\cite{onecycle}; the open RTL-to-GDSII pipeline is
LibreLane~\cite{librelane} on sky130A~\cite{sky130_pdk} with
OpenROAD~\cite{openroad}, Yosys~\cite{yosys}, and OpenRAM
macros~\cite{openram}.

The contributions of TINKER are threefold. First, a fully-digital
threshold-logic decomposition of the XNOR-popcount-threshold core,
mapping the BNN inner product onto \texttt{sky130\_fd\_sc\_hd}
standard cells via a chunked LUT scheme --- in contrast to prior
threshold-logic accelerators that depend on mixed-signal flash or
current-mode cells~\cite{tulip}. Second, a complete SoC integration
that hosts an entire grouped binary MLP on a single sixteen-neuron
tile through five sequential evaluations, controlled by a PicoRV32
RV32I core over a Wishbone B4 fabric with OpenRAM IMEM/DMEM macros. Third, a system-level power finding
that the TLG tile constitutes 24.3\,\% of total SoC power once
memory is properly architected (47.21\,mW total at activity-aware
sign-off), up from 10.81\,\% under a register-file baseline at
byte-identical tile RTL --- exposing the BNN datapath as the
first-order system-level optimisation target. End-to-end MNIST
inference reaches 85.60\,\% accuracy with a 95\,\% Wilson confidence
interval of \([84.90\,\%,\,86.27\,\%]\) on the full 10\,000-image
test set, at 8.06\,\textmu J per image.

Section~\ref{sec:background} surveys related digital BNN
accelerators and open-flow neural-inference work.
Section~\ref{sec:arch} describes the SoC architecture and tile
microarchitecture. Section~\ref{sec:impl} covers the open-flow
implementation, sign-off methodology, and BNN deployment.
Section~\ref{sec:eval} presents functional verification, the
activity-aware power decomposition, energy and accuracy results, and
the comparison against prior digital BNN ASICs.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the system-level findings and
limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

We review the digital BNN accelerator landscape against which this
design is positioned, then turn to the threshold-logic architectural
lineage that defines its closest comparator.
YodaNN~\cite{yodann2018} pairs a binary-weight datapath with
standard-cell memories and aggressive voltage scaling in UMC 65\,nm to
push AlexNet-class CNNs into the ultralow-power regime.
XNE~\cite{xne2018} integrates an XNOR-popcount engine with a hybrid
SRAM/SCM memory subsystem alongside a microcontroller in 22\,nm,
reporting 21.6\,fJ/op for fully-binary inference.
ChewBaccaNN~\cite{chewbaccann2021} couples a flexible
XNOR-and-popcount-tree datapath with body-biasing and 0.4\,V operation
in GF 22\,nm FDX, reaching 223\,TOPS/W at peak.
The closest architectural comparator is TULIP, which takes the
threshold-logic-gate route in 40\,nm by mapping
XNOR-popcount-threshold neurons onto a network of programmable
threshold-logic standard cells whose threshold compare uses embedded
flash devices~\cite{tulip}. Our design shares the threshold-logic
philosophy but commits to a fully-digital decomposition: the same
XNOR-popcount-threshold core is mapped entirely onto
\texttt{sky130\_fd\_sc\_hd} multiplexers and AOI cells, with no
analog or mixed-signal devices, preserving portability across
digital PDKs.

At the system level, the closest comparator is
OpenSpike~\cite{openspike}, which integrates a spiking neural
network with a PicoRV32 controller and OpenRAM macros on sky130 ---
a useful precedent for SoC-scale neural inference on this
substrate, but occupying the SNN rather than BNN computational
regime. The system contribution of this work is not the use of
threshold logic per se, nor the choice of digital cells, but the
SoC-level integration that hosts an entire grouped binary MLP on a
single tile RTL through software-scheduled sequential evaluations,
and the system-level power decomposition that exposes where
optimisation should focus once memory is no longer dominant.
FPGA-side BNN frameworks such as FINN~\cite{finn} occupy a separate
design point that we do not directly compare against. With the
comparator landscape established, the following sections describe
the architecture (Section~\ref{sec:arch}), implementation
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}), and evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\subsection{System overview}
\label{sec:arch:overview}

TINKER, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:soc-block}, is a single-clock RV32I
system whose role is to host the TLG-mapped BNN tile under software
control, with all peripheral state and inference scheduling driven
by the on-chip processor rather than by hardware sequencers
external to the tile. A PicoRV32 core~\cite{picorv32} acts as the lone Wishbone B4
classic master and drives a single-cycle bus with 32-bit address and
32-bit data. Address decode on \texttt{adr[31:28]} dispatches to one of
four word-aligned slaves: instruction memory (IMEM at
\texttt{0x0000\_0000}), data memory (DMEM at \texttt{0x1000\_0000}),
the tile peripheral (TILE at \texttt{0x2000\_0000}), and a sim-observ%
ability GPIO (GPIO at \texttt{0x3000\_0000}). Unmapped accesses raise
an error so that wild pointers hang visibly rather than silently. Reset
is asynchronously asserted and synchronously deasserted through a
two-stage flop chain shared by every block; firmware boots from
\texttt{PROGADDR\_RESET\,=\,0x0000\_0000} and runs bare-metal, with
the stack pointer initialised to the top of DMEM.

The architecture is deliberately minimal. There is no interrupt
controller, no MMIO timer, no off-chip bus, and no DMA --- the BNN
inference handshake is polled (Section~\ref{sec:arch:wrapper}), so an
asynchronous-event subsystem would be dead area. The choice of a
single-master Wishbone fabric over a crossbar is the same calculus: a
one-master, four-slave decode is roughly 100 lines of synthesisable
Verilog, well within the open-flow toolchain's comfort zone, and adds
no arbitration latency to the tightest instruction-fetch path. The
system clock is nominally 100\,MHz, matching the SDC inherited from the
standalone tile characterisation.

\subsection{TLG-mapped BNN tile}
\label{sec:arch:tile}

The tile, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tile-microarch}, computes a
sixteen-output binary inner product against a 64-bit input vector each
evaluation. Sixteen parallel neurons share a registered input
\(x \in \{-1,+1\}^{64}\), encoded as a single 64-bit word, and each
holds its own 64-bit weight \(w_i\) and 7-bit threshold \(\theta_i\) in
a configuration regfile. Per neuron, the binary inner product collapses
to an XNOR followed by a population count~\cite{xnornet}: the output
bit is
\(y_i = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\,\mathrm{popcount}\!\left(x \xnor w_i\right) \ge \theta_i\,\right]\).
Sixteen neurons evaluated in parallel produce a 16-bit binary vector
per cycle of throughput, with a two-cycle latency (input-register cycle
plus combinational evaluate-and-register cycle).

The popcount is mapped to TLG-encoded threshold logic by partitioning
the 64-bit XNOR output into eleven chunks --- ten 6-bit chunks plus one
4-bit residue --- and looking up each chunk's population count in a
Verilog \texttt{case} table. Yosys lowers each table into a
technology-mapped network of \texttt{sky130\_fd\_sc\_hd} multiplexers
and AOI cells that implements the underlying threshold function as a
fan-in-bounded combinational tree. The TLG decomposition handles each
6-bit chunk's population count; the eleven 3-bit subcounts are then
summed by a conventional width-seven adder tree and compared against
\(\theta_i\). This is the principal contrast with the
threshold-logic processor TULIP~\cite{tulip}, which depends on
programmable flash devices for its threshold compare and is therefore
tied to a specific mixed-signal flow. Because every chunk LUT and
the surrounding adder tree resolve to digital standard cells, the
same RTL ports unmodified to any digital PDK with a comparable cell
library, including open-source ones.

The configuration regfile holds 16 \(\times\) 64-bit weight words and
16 \(\times\) 7-bit thresholds, addressed through a 6-bit
\texttt{cfg\_addr}: bits \texttt{[5:4]\,=\,00} index a weight,
\texttt{[5:4]\,=\,01} index a threshold, and \texttt{[3:0]} select the
neuron. Weights are loaded once per network rather than per inference,
so the cfg port carries traffic only at boot. A dedicated
\texttt{x\_valid} strobe latches the input register and starts the
two-cycle pipeline; \texttt{y\_valid} pulses on the cycle the result
leaves the output register.

\subsection{Tile wrapper and inference handshake}
\label{sec:arch:wrapper}

The Wishbone-to-tile bridge \texttt{wb\_tile\_wrapper} occupies a
512-byte slave window and exposes the tile's cfg port and
\(x_\text{in}/y_\text{out}\) handshake as memory-mapped registers.
Weights are committed by a two-phase write: a 32-bit \texttt{W[i].LO}
write loads a per-neuron shadow register, and a subsequent
\texttt{W[i].HI} write to the next word offset drives one cycle of
\texttt{cfg\_we} carrying the assembled 64-bit value. Thresholds are
atomic single-word writes. Inputs use the same two-phase commit
(\texttt{XIN.LO}/\texttt{XIN.HI}); writing \texttt{XIN.HI} additionally
pulses \texttt{x\_valid} for one cycle and asserts \texttt{STATUS.BUSY}.
Firmware polls \texttt{STATUS} until \texttt{BUSY} clears, reads
\texttt{YOUT}, and writes \texttt{CTRL.CLR\_DONE} to arm the next
inference. Writes to weight, threshold, or input registers while
\texttt{BUSY\,=\,1} are silently dropped: the bus access acks normally
but no shadow is updated, which causes a misbehaving program to hang
visibly rather than corrupt an in-flight evaluation. This is a
deliberate testability choice: a silently corrupted inference is a
far worse failure mode than an observably hung firmware loop. The wrapper
carries roughly 1.3\,K flops, of which approximately 700 exist purely
to make every writable register read-back-able --- a deliberate
testability deviation from the wrapper specification.

The memory subsystem instantiates two pre-hardened OpenRAM~\cite{openram}
macros: \texttt{sky130\_sram\_2kbyte\_1rw1r\_32x512\_8} as the IMEM and
\texttt{sky130\_sram\_1kbyte\_1rw1r\_32x256\_8} as the DMEM. Both
macros are wrapped by a small two-state Wishbone FSM that inserts one
wait state on reads to cover the macro's registered \texttt{dout0}
latency. The motivation for moving from a register-file baseline to
OpenRAM macros is power: in the pre-OpenRAM version, the IMEM
read mux dominated both die area and switching power, the SoC drew
106\,mW, and the tile accounted for only 10.81\,\% of system power.
Replacing the register files with macros drops total power to
47.21\,mW and lifts the tile's share to 24.29\,\%, which is the
principal reason the tile becomes a meaningful optimisation target at
the system level (Section~\ref{sec:eval:power}).

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Open-flow toolchain}
\label{sec:impl:flow}

The SoC is implemented through the LibreLane 3.0.3 RTL-to-GDSII
flow~\cite{librelane} on the SkyWater sky130A open-source
PDK~\cite{sky130_pdk}, in the OpenLane lineage~\cite{openlane}.
Yosys~\cite{yosys} synthesises the design against the
\texttt{sky130\_fd\_sc\_hd} standard-cell library;
OpenROAD~\cite{openroad} drives floorplanning, placement, clock-tree
synthesis, routing, and antenna repair; Magic and KLayout perform GDS
streamout and DRC; Netgen performs LVS. The two memories are
pre-hardened OpenRAM~\cite{openram} macros ---
\texttt{sky130\_sram\_2kbyte\_1rw1r\_32x512\_8}
(683\,\(\times\)\,417\,\textmu m) for the IMEM and
\texttt{sky130\_sram\_1kbyte\_1rw1r\_32x256\_8}
(480\,\(\times\)\,398\,\textmu m) for the DMEM --- manually placed
against a 1\,500\,\(\times\)\,1\,500\,\textmu m absolute floorplan with
PDN hookups to the sky130 dual rail (\texttt{vccd1}/\texttt{vssd1}).
Macro integration required iteration on placement constraints, an
OpenSTA blackbox stub for the behavioural macro views, and removal of
the wrapper-level \texttt{keep\_hierarchy} attributes to satisfy
LibreLane's flat-macro convention. The full RTL-to-GDSII pipeline
is driven from a single \texttt{config.json}, with multi-corner STA,
sign-off, and activity-aware power decomposition reproducible from
the same artifact.

Fig.~\ref{fig:layout} shows the post-P\&R floorplan, with the IMEM
and DMEM macros along the upper edge of the die and the std-cell logic
below. Multi-corner static timing was run across nine corners covering
the three sky130 process points (TT, FF, SS), three temperatures
($-40$\,\textdegree{}C, 25\,\textdegree{}C, 100\,\textdegree{}C), and
three supply voltages (1.60\,V, 1.80\,V, 1.95\,V). At the headline
\texttt{nom\_tt\_025C\_1v80} corner the design closes with
$+0.51$\,ns setup slack and $+0.37$\,ns hold slack; hold timing is
clean across all nine corners. The slow-corner setup violations at
\texttt{nom\_ss\_100C\_1v60} are an attribute of the OpenRAM macros'
single TT-corner Liberty --- a documented LibreLane 3.x limitation
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, not a design defect.
Physical sign-off is clean on the four authoritative checks: KLayout
DRC reports 0 errors (the eight-million Magic DRC flags inside the
macro footprint are a known sky130 deficiency around hardened SRAM
internals and are conventionally ignored), Netgen LVS 0 errors,
KLayout XOR against the Magic-streamed GDS 0 differences across all
108 layers, and antenna 0 violating nets and 0 violating pins.

\subsection{BNN deployment}
\label{sec:impl:bnn}

With sign-off established, we describe the network deployed onto
the SoC. The deployed network is a grouped binary MLP over a
14\,\(\times\)\,14 input. After a 2\,\(\times\)\,2 average pool and a
per-quadrant median binarisation, four parallel branches each map a
49-bit quadrant to 16 hidden bits in one tile evaluation; the four
16-bit outputs concatenate into a 64-bit hidden vector that the same
tile maps to ten output logits in a fifth evaluation. Grouping
(rather than full $196 \to 64$ connectivity) is a hardware-shape
choice: each branch fits one tile-evaluation fanin, so the entire
network maps onto the SoC without changing tile or wrapper RTL.
The accuracy gap from full $196 \to 64$ connectivity to grouped
$4 \times (49 \to 16)$, measured against an unconstrained-fanin
training run on the same data with the same training recipe, is
0.87\,pp --- well below the run-to-run accuracy variance observed
across the three training seeds, and below the 0.69\,pp half-width
of the 95\,\% Wilson confidence interval on the deployed network's
MNIST accuracy.
Per-image inference therefore costs four hidden-layer batches plus
one output-layer batch, which the PicoRV32 firmware schedules
sequentially against the cfg-write/poll/read protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:arch:wrapper}, totalling 17\,068 cycles or
170.7\,\textmu s at 100\,MHz. The bare-metal firmware compiles to
roughly 1.5\,KB of RV32I and shares the IMEM with the binary weights;
per-image input bits are preloaded into DMEM by the testbench at
simulation time zero. A vectorised Python integer model serves as
the deployment golden, folding the trained batch-norm parameters into
per-neuron thresholds via
$t_i = \lceil (49 + \mathrm{sgn}(\gamma_i)\,\mu_i - \beta_i\sigma_i/|\gamma_i|)/2 \rceil$;
SoC RTL simulation matches this golden on all sixteen images of the
verification set. Network weights are trained off-chip with binary-weight
gradient descent~\cite{binaryconnect}, a hard-tanh
straight-through estimator~\cite{hubara_bnn}, a OneCycleLR
schedule~\cite{onecycle}, 30 epochs, and a three-seed sweep (seeds
42, 1042, 2042) with the best-validation seed deployed.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Functional verification}
\label{sec:eval:verif}

The deployed network is exercised through four verification gates,
summarised in Table~\ref{tab:verification}. The vectorised Python
integer model \texttt{bnn\_reference.py} is treated as the deployment
golden, since its arithmetic is exact in the same fixed-width integer
domain as the on-chip XNOR-popcount; the trained PyTorch model is a
training-time proxy whose floating-point activations may differ near
batch-norm thresholds. The 16-image verification set is the first 16 images of the
MNIST test split, used as a fast inner-loop bit-exact check; the
full 10\,000-image test set is covered by the integer-golden gate.
PyTorch and the integer golden agree on 15 of these 16 images. The single mismatch (image~9: PyTorch labels
9, golden and SoC both label 7) lies on a folded-batch-norm activation
boundary near zero and is the expected behaviour of the integer
quantisation, not a bug. The post-P\&R SoC RTL simulation then matches
the integer golden bit-exact on all sixteen images. On the full
10\,000-image MNIST test set~\cite{mnist}, the same network reaches
85.60\,\% accuracy (8\,560 correct), with a 95\,\% Wilson confidence interval of

\subsection{Activity-aware power decomposition}
\label{sec:eval:power}

Activity-aware power is reported at the headline
\texttt{max\_ff\_n40C\_1v95} corner. The full sixteen-image inference
testbench is replayed against the post-P\&R netlist, the chip-pin VCD
is annotated through OpenSTA's \texttt{read\_vcd}, supplemented with
forty-seven named-net activities for the major interior wires, and
\texttt{report\_power} is decomposed into per-instance buckets by
\texttt{scripts/bucket\_soc\_cells.py}. Total post-P\&R power is
47.21\,mW. Fig.~\ref{fig:power_breakdown} shows the hierarchical
decomposition. The clock tree is the largest single contributor at
15.10\,mW (32.0\,\%). The TLG-mapped tile is the largest active
datapath bucket at 11.47\,mW (24.3\,\%); the PicoRV32 core draws
7.71\,mW (16.3\,\%); the IMEM and DMEM macros draw 5.56\,mW
(11.8\,\%) and 3.85\,mW (8.2\,\%) respectively; shared logic and the
GPIO slave account for 1.24\,mW (2.6\,\%) and 0.30\,mW (0.6\,\%).

The actionable insight is the change in the tile's share of the
budget. With the register-file IMEM/DMEM of the Phase~3 baseline, the
same tile RTL --- byte-identical post-flatten netlist --- contributed
11.47\,mW of an SoC total of 106.06\,mW, or 10.81\,\%. Replacing the
register files with OpenRAM macros drops the system total to
47.21\,mW while leaving the tile bucket unchanged, lifting the tile's
share to 24.29\,\% and dropping the memory subsystem's combined share
from 36.4\,\% to 19.9\,\%. The system-level finding is that the same hierarchical
activity-aware analysis exposes the tile as the largest active
block once memory is no longer dominant, redirecting the
optimisation target from the memory subsystem onto the inference
datapath.

\subsection{Energy and throughput}
\label{sec:eval:energy}

At the same \texttt{max\_ff} corner, the SoC executes 17\,068 cycles
per image, i.e.\ 170.7\,\textmu s and \textbf{8.06\,\textmu J per
  inference} at 100\,MHz. This corresponds to \textbf{124\,113
  inferences/J} and a sustained throughput of 5\,860\,inf/s under
continuous polling. The companion \texttt{nom\_tt\_025C\_1v80} corner
is slightly more efficient at an estimated 7.09\,\textmu J/image and
141\,044\,inf/J. Compared with the Phase~3 7\,\(\times\)\,7
deployment on the same SoC, the grouped 14\,\(\times\)\,14 network
gains $+14.48$\,pp of accuracy at $+1.0\,\%$ energy overhead per
image: the post-P\&R netlist, sign-off artefacts, and clock are
byte-identical, and only the firmware DMEM layout (8 vs 2 words/image)
and the binary weight image change between the two deployments. The
accuracy-per-microjoule figure of merit therefore rises from
8.91\,pp/\textmu J to 10.62\,pp/\textmu J, a 19.2\,\% improvement at
unchanged silicon.

\subsection{Comparison with prior digital BNN ASICs}
\label{sec:eval:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} positions the proposed SoC against
representative digital BNN ASICs. The aim of the comparison is not to
overtake the leading raw-throughput-per-watt baselines:
XNE~\cite{xne2018}, YodaNN~\cite{yodann2018}, and
ChewBaccaNN~\cite{chewbaccann2021} are 22--65\,nm commercial-flow
accelerators, and at those nodes a BNN datapath enjoys node-and-voltage
advantages that no sky130A 1.8\,V design can close. The contribution
of the present work sits in a different category, defined by two
simultaneous constraints: a fully open RTL-to-GDSII pipeline
(LibreLane + sky130A + OpenRAM) and a fully-digital TLG decomposition
mapped into the standard-cell library, with a soft RV32I core
orchestrating end-to-end inference at the SoC level.

The primary architectural comparator is TULIP~\cite{tulip}. TULIP
shares the threshold-logic philosophy but commits to a mixed-signal
flow with programmable flash devices for the threshold compare,
which ties the design to a specific PDK family. The present design
demonstrates that the same TLG mapping can be carried entirely by
digital standard cells, preserving portability across any digital
PDK. The secondary comparator is OpenSpike~\cite{openspike}, which
shares the SoC-scale neural-inference-on-sky130 substrate (PicoRV32
+ OpenRAM) but targets a spiking rather than a binary network,
occupying a complementary computational regime.
The figures reported in
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} are end-to-end at the SoC level --- CPU,
memories, clock tree, and tile included --- which is conservative
against the IP-block measurements that several baselines report and
makes the per-inference numbers directly comparable for a
system-integration audience.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The most consequential finding is the change in where SoC power is
spent. With the register-file IMEM/DMEM of the Phase~3 baseline, the
TLG tile contributed 11.47\,mW of an SoC total of 106.06\,mW
(10.81\,\%) and the memory subsystem alone consumed 36.4\,\% of the
budget. Substituting OpenRAM macros leaves the tile bucket
byte-identical at 11.47\,mW but drops the system total to 47.21\,mW,
lifting the tile to 24.29\,\% and shrinking the memory share to
19.9\,\%. The system-integration consequence is that BNN compute is
no longer hidden by storage overhead at SoC scale, and tile-level
optimisation becomes a first-order target rather than a rounding
error against IMEM/DMEM dominance.

The reported per-inference energy of 8.06\,\textmu J is orders of
magnitude above what advanced-node commercial-flow accelerators
report, and the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:eval:comparison}
does not chase that gap. The system-level claim is architectural
rather than energy-efficiency: that a fully-digital threshold-logic
decomposition, integrated as a complete SoC with software-scheduled
sequential tile evaluations, exposes the BNN datapath as the
dominant active power bucket once memory is no longer dominant.
The energy regime sits where a 130\,nm digital-cell-based
design must, and the architectural contribution is what carries
forward to nodes where energy efficiency is the bottleneck.

The reported numbers are conservative on two methodological axes.
The OpenRAM macros ship Liberty for the TT\_1p8V\_25C corner only, so
all multi-corner STA passes use that single macro Liberty; the
slow-corner setup violations at \texttt{nom\_ss\_100C\_1v60} are a
documented LibreLane 3.x limitation rather than silicon defects, and
the macro-internal-power figure does not track corner. The headline
power number is taken at \texttt{max\_ff\_n40C\_1v95} and is
activity-aware (chip-pin VCD plus interior named-net annotations),
which is the conservative direction for an inference workload. The
present design does not demonstrate a multi-frequency power sweep, a
larger network beyond grouped 14\,\(\times\)\,14, or a tape-out;
those extensions are out of scope here rather than failures of the
approach.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented TINKER, a configurable threshold-logic-mapped
BNN inference SoC. The XNOR-popcount-threshold core is decomposed
into a chunked LUT scheme that lowers entirely onto
\texttt{sky130\_fd\_sc\_hd} standard cells, integrated as a
sixteen-neuron tile alongside a PicoRV32 RV32I core, a Wishbone B4
fabric, and OpenRAM IMEM/DMEM macros. An entire grouped binary MLP
maps onto the tile RTL through five software-scheduled sequential
evaluations, reaching 85.60\,\% MNIST accuracy at 8.06\,\textmu J
per inference and 47.21\,mW activity-aware power. The system-level
power decomposition exposes the TLG tile as the dominant active
bucket at 24.3\,\%, redirecting the optimisation target from the
memory subsystem onto the inference datapath itself. Tile-level
optimisation, larger networks, and tape-out become the natural
next steps.
